The party ends. The rental doesn’t.

Men’s Wearhouse and Jos. A. Bank want wedding rentals back the day after the event, at a store during open hours, or dropped at the shipper on the label the first business day. Miss it and the late fee is $20 a day, up to replacement cost. Sunday closings and outbound flights are where groomsmen get burned.

In-store orders are due back the day after the wedding, during store hours. A Saturday wedding often means a Sunday errand at a closed shop, then a Monday argument about two late days.

Two ways in

Locker at the venue, or a pickup with a signature.

Path A · their original idea

Drop it in the locker.

  • A bank of lockers lives at the venue, near the exit or the groomsmen’s quarters.
  • Guest pays ahead, agrees to terms on a QR code, and lists every piece.
  • At the end of the night they bag it, photograph it, and confirm the list.
  • Morning route clears the lockers and hits the rental shops.
Path B · the safer first version

Someone shows up.

  • No locker hardware. A scheduled window, like a ride pickup.
  • Operator and guest check the list together and both sign on the phone.
  • Missing studs get caught while everyone is still in the room.
  • Same shop return. Better chain of custody. Easier to pilot this month.
The part that keeps you out of small claims

Nothing is “in the locker” unless the photo says so.

The failure mode is obvious: a guest swears the cufflinks went in, the bag comes back light, and the $50 job now owes a replacement. The flow below is the product. Push notifications are how the guest knows it landed.

List the pieces before the wedding

Jacket, trousers, shirt, shoes, tie, cummerbund, studs, cufflinks. Only what they declare is in scope.

QR code, terms, once

They agree the operator is a courier, not the rental company. Damage, stains, and the rental contract stay with the renter.

Photo and a checkbox at drop

Each declared item gets confirmed in the bag. No confirmation, no handoff. That record is the defense if something is “missing.”

Ping when the shop has it

Text or push at pickup, at the shop door, and when the return is accepted. The guest should not have to wonder.

Open before anyone buys a locker

Five things the sample does not pretend are solved.

  1. Will the shop take a third-party drop? Men’s Wearhouse can take a return at any of their stores, but the contract is with the renter. A pilot has to confirm they’ll accept a courier with the rental agreement and a release. If they won’t, the product is a labeled ship-back, not a counter drop.
  2. Sunday hours. A Saturday wedding due “the next day” collides with closed stores. The service has to know whether Monday morning is on time or already a $20 day. That answer changes the promise.
  3. Bailee, not buddy. Once the bag is in the locker, lost or stolen pieces are an insurance question. Cap liability at declared items, photograph the handoff, and don’t hold goods overnight without a policy that names it.
  4. Drunk inventory. Shoes left by the dance floor are not in the locker. The checklist is the product. If it isn’t confirmed, it isn’t picked up.
  5. Mail-back already exists. Shipped Men’s Wearhouse orders include a return label. The wedge is in-store parties, mixed flight times, and anyone who will not find a drop box before the shuttle.

Courier terms, short version

Ready Suit Go moves declared items from the venue to the rental shop. It does not replace the rental contract. Stains, tears, and missing undeclared pieces stay with the renter. Handoff counts only after the photo confirmation. A lost declared item is capped at the shop’s replacement schedule, and only if the photo shows it in the bag.
